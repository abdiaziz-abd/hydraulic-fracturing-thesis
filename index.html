Fracturing Under Watch

Optimizing hydraulic fracturing through micro-seismic monitoring for asset preservation and value enhancement. Every treatment sends tiny seismic signals through the rock — this thesis asks whether reading them in real time, with machine learning, changes the outcome.

Abdiaziz Mohamed Abdi · Cyprus International University

Stage 01 · Setting

Fracturing the Unconventional

Hydraulic fracturing has become the defining technique for unlocking hydrocarbons trapped in unconventional and naturally fractured reservoirs. High-pressure fluid creates and extends a network of induced fractures, connecting the wellbore to previously unreachable oil and gas.

Every fracture treatment carries risk. As fluid pressure builds underground, it releases tiny seismic events — far too small to feel, but large enough to map.

Across thousands of monitored fracture stages in North America, the largest recorded microseismic event never exceeded a magnitude of +1.0 — roughly three orders of magnitude below what a person could ever feel at the surface.

Shale stimulation typically stays in the -4 to -1 magnitude range, with fault-interaction events rarely climbing more than one or two magnitudes higher.
Source: Warpinski 2013 · Thesis Ch. 1
Stage 02 · Gap

Why Traditional Models Fall Short

Conventional fracture-design software estimates a simple, symmetrical fracture before the pumps even start. In naturally fractured carbonate reservoirs, reality rarely cooperates.

Induced fractures interact unpredictably with pre-existing natural fractures and faults, producing complex networks analytical models can't anticipate. This research integrates micro-seismic monitoring and machine learning directly into fracturing evaluation, aimed at two outcomes: preserving the asset and enhancing its long-term value.

Source: Thesis Ch. 1, Sections 1.2–1.4
Stage 03 · Method

From Signal to Insight

Raw microseismic data arrives noisy, sparse, and often incomplete. This study applies a machine-learning-enhanced pipeline: noise is classified and suppressed, events are detected and picked, their locations and source mechanisms resolved, and the resulting event cloud interpreted as a fracture network.

The methodology was validated against two real-world settings: the Gagarinskoe and Ozernoe carbonate oilfields in Perm Krai, Russia, and a horizontal shale gas well — Well X — in the Puguang territory, China.

Source: Thesis Ch. 2–3, Fig. 3-1
Finding 01

Complexity Wins

Well 1, in the Gagarinskoe field, developed a simple, symmetrical fracture. Production jumped from 1.4 to 9.0 tons/day — but faded within 11 months as the unpropped fracture closed under overburden pressure.

Well 2 developed a multi-branched fracture network intersecting natural fissures. Production rose from 2.0 to 12.5 tons/day and stayed elevated for 18 months with only minimal decline.

The difference tracks the storativity ratio (ω) — how much fluid-storage volume the fracture network provides.

Complex fracture networks sustained enhanced production for over 60% longer than simple, single-plane fractures in the same field.
Source: Thesis §3.2.4, Fig. 3.7–3.11
Finding 02

Matching the Model to the Problem

Not every architecture solves the same monitoring challenge. Distinct model families cluster around specific tasks:

  • C

    CNNs dominate noise classification, event detection, and picking.

  • P

    Physics-informed networks suit localization and source-mechanism inversion where velocity models are poorly constrained.

  • G

    Graph & clustering models excel at grouping events into fracture-network clusters.

Source: Thesis Ch. 2, §2.2–2.8
Finding 03

The Complexity Premium

Comparing Well 1 and Well 2 side by side makes the pattern unmistakable: the complex-network well outperformed the simple one on every production metric — flow-rate increase, duration of enhanced production, and post-frac output.

Source: Thesis §3.2.4, Tables 3.2–3.4
Stage 08 · Conclusion

A Framework for the Field

Pair every hydraulic fracturing job with real-time micro-seismic monitoring, let machine learning carry the burden of noise suppression and event interpretation, and design fracture treatments around each reservoir's specific geology.

  1. Reduce environmental risk through geology-aware fracture design.
  2. Extend productive well life by favoring complex, well-propped networks.
  3. Standardize ML-assisted monitoring as routine, not a specialized add-on.
Source: Thesis Ch. 4, Recommendations 1–6
