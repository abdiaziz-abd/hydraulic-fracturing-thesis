Fracturing Under Watch

Optimizing hydraulic fracturing through micro-seismic monitoring for asset preservation and value enhancement

Abdiaziz Mohamed Abdi · Cyprus International University · Nicosia, 2025

Every fracture treatment shakes the rock a little. Those tremors are a thousand times too small to feel — but large enough to map. This thesis asks a simple question with an expensive answer: if you listen to them in real time, and let machine learning do the listening, does the well produce more?

Section 01 · Setting

Cracking rock you cannot see

Hydraulic fracturing is now the default technique for pulling hydrocarbons out of tight, unconventional and naturally fractured formations. Fluid is pumped until the rock fails, and a network of fissures opens a path from the reservoir to the wellbore.

In carbonate reservoirs the process refuses to behave. Their pore structure carries both primary voids and secondary cavities, fractures and fissures — so an advancing hydraulic fracture meets natural ones and crosses, deflects, or localises, leaking fluid into side paths as it goes.

Nobody can see this happen. What can be observed is the sound of it: as injection raises formation pressure, strain energy piles up around the fracture tip until the rock breaks and releases elastic waves. That is a micro-seismic event, and a dense enough cloud of them draws the fracture.

At least half of all micro-seismic events occur after the fracture tip has already passed the event location — evidence that much of the signal comes from interaction with natural fractures, not from the tip itself.
Source: Thesis §1.1, §1.5 · after Warpinski (2013), Fig. 1-2
Section 02 · The signal

A millionth of the energy you put in

Induced events fall roughly between magnitude −2.0 and +2.5; shale stimulation itself sits in the −4 to −1 band. Across thousands of monitored stages in North America, the largest micro-seism ever recorded did not exceed +1.0 — about three orders of magnitude below anything a person could feel at surface.

The energy accounting is starker still. Total detectable micro-seismic energy released during a treatment is on the order of hundreds of kilojoules. The treatment itself injects hundreds of millions of kilojoules. The seismic response is roughly one part in a million.

The same asymmetry applies to volume: summed micro-seismic displacement amounts to a few litres against hundreds or thousands of cubic metres of fluid pumped. This is why the event cloud must be read as a map of where the rock moved, never as a direct measure of stimulated reservoir volume.

The Gutenberg–Richter b-value sits near 1.0 for natural earthquakes but ranges 1.0 – 2.0 for fracturing-induced events: proportionally far more small events, far fewer large ones.
Source: Thesis §1.2, §1.5.1 · Figs. 1-3, 1-5
Section 03 · Environment

The distance to the water table

The loudest public objection to fracturing is that fractures reach drinking water. Monitoring data answers it directly, because every stage's shallowest and deepest detected event is recorded.

Across thousands of fractures in six major North American shale basins — Barnett, Marcellus, Woodford, Eagle Ford, Haynesville and Muskwa/Evie — vertical growth is real but small next to the separation from near-surface aquifers. Stress contrasts, material changes between layers, horizontal bedding interfaces and high-permeability streaks all act as barriers that spread growth sideways instead of up.

The spikes that do appear in the record are fault interactions. A fault intersected by the treatment typically raises event magnitude by one to two units, and can steer growth up, down or sideways — which is precisely why it shows up on the monitor.

The fluid volume required for a fracture to climb from treatment depth to a water well far exceeds anything pumped in a real job, before the geological barriers are even counted.
Source: Thesis §1.5.2 · Figs. 1-4, 1-5
Section 04 · The gap

Where the design model breaks

Fracture-design software predicts a fracture before the pumps start. Analytical models are only accurate under idealised conditions; numerical models are constrained by small samples and laboratory scale, and both lean on an initial, frequently imprecise estimate of recovery. No existing method can confirm on its own that a fracture network formed at all.

The three families of diagnostics each fall short somewhere. Inclinometer surveys depend heavily on instrument placement and lose accuracy as networks get complex. Near-well tools — compass logging, PLT, image logs — see fracture height at the wellbore and nothing about length. Indirect methods invert pressure and rate data, and that inverse problem has no unique solution.

Micro-seismic monitoring is the exception that is field-scale, direct and largely insensitive to instrument placement. Its own weakness is resolution: location error grows with source–receiver distance, from roughly 16 m at 305 m to as much as 350 m at 900 m.

In carbonate reservoirs, roughly 80% of treatments produce complex geometry — but the design software anticipated a single symmetric fracture in every one of them.
Source: Thesis §1.2, §1.3, §3.2.4 · Table 3.2
Section 05 · Method

From raw signal to fracture network

Downhole recordings arrive buried in low-frequency background noise that conventional bandpass filtering cannot remove. Worse, "dry" events — slip on pre-existing fractures away from the active hydraulic fracture — look almost identical to genuine signal because they are generated the same way. Left in, they inflate the apparent stimulated volume.

The pipeline this thesis assembles runs noise classification and suppression first, then detection and picking, then localisation and source-mechanism inversion, and finally clusters the surviving events into a fracture-network interpretation. Machine learning is inserted at every stage rather than bolted on at the end.

Three field settings validate it: the Gagarinskoe and Ozernoe carbonate oilfields in Perm Krai, Russia, monitored with ground-based receiver arrays; Well X, a horizontal shale-gas well in the Puguang territory of China's Sichuan Basin; and the Qusaiba shale of north-western Saudi Arabia, monitored from a downhole array in a neighbouring well.

Section 06 · Machine learning

Matching the model to the task

Chapter Two surveys ML across the whole monitoring chain, and the useful result is that architectures do not compete — they specialise.

  • C
    CNNs dominate noise classification, event detection and phase picking, where the input is an image-like waveform patch.
  • P
    Physics-informed networks take localisation and source-mechanism inversion, embedding the wave equation so predictions stay physically admissible where velocity models are poorly constrained.
  • G
    Graph, clustering and autoencoder models group events into fracture-network clusters and reconstruct gapped wavefields.
  • B
    Bayesian and mixture-density models return a distribution rather than a point, which is what source-mechanism work actually needs.

Denoising is the clearest case. DeepDenoiser learns signal and noise masks from amplitude spectra; UrbanDenoiser is the same architecture re-tuned on a dense urban array by transfer learning, and it recovered mantle seismicity under Los Angeles that the original model missed. For microseismic data, where clean reference traces do not exist, variational autoencoders and blind-spot networks train on the noisy records themselves — the VAE approach was demonstrated on hydraulic fracturing of the Marcellus shale and beat both f–x deconvolution and damped multichannel singular spectrum analysis.

Three obstacles remain honest ones: labelled seismic data is scarce and site-specific, deep models are hard to interpret in a domain where the reasoning must be auditable, and training cost is real.

Source: Thesis Ch. 2, §2.2–2.9 · after Anikiev et al. (2023), Earth-Science Reviews 239, 104371
Section 07 · Adoption

A field that took off in 2015

The idea of putting machine learning on micro-seismic data is not new — the first attempts date to the early 1990s. What was missing was the hardware and the tooling. A review of 222 publications spanning 1990 to 2022 shows almost nothing for two decades, then two step changes.

The first is 2006, when the first unified graphics-and-compute GPU architecture programmable in C arrived and general-purpose GPU computing became practical. The second is 2015, the first public release of the Keras deep-learning library, after which the publication count grows exponentially.

The order in which applications appear is the ordinary shape of technology adoption. Detection and localisation — the fundamentals — come first. Noise suppression and classification, source-mechanism determination and cluster analysis arrive years later, once the basics are reliable enough to build on.

Micro-seismic monitoring has a handicap earthquake seismology does not: monitoring usually begins when the operation begins, so there is no historical catalogue from the site to train on. Synthetic training data is not a shortcut here — it is generally the only option.
Source: Anikiev et al. (2023), Fig. 1 · Thesis Ch. 2
Section 08 · Detection

Three ways to see an event

Detection is where machine learning displaced classical processing first, because the classical options both fail in a known way. STA/LTA is fast and general but insensitive to time-varying noise and low-magnitude events, and its answer depends heavily on the thresholds chosen. Template matching detects far smaller events but only those resembling the master event, and it is expensive enough to rule out real-time use.

Recast as computer vision, the problem splits into three framings that return different amounts of information: binary window classification (is there an event in this window at all), bounding-box detection (which time range and which offsets), and semantic segmentation (label every sample of the record as arrival or not).

The reported gains are large. A CNN trained on mining-induced events detected ten times more events than a human expert. A YOLOv3 network trained on semi-synthetic data recovered over 80% of manually picked events across 3,800 DAS channels of a four-hour record, with the misses concentrated at low SNR. Treating an ocean-bottom-node array as an image and segmenting it with a U-Net ran in real time on a single GPU.

For phase picking, the leading models — PhaseNet, EQTransformer and generalized phase detection — benchmark to roughly equivalent performance. The remaining gains come from using the array rather than the trace: exploiting coherence between stations raised the F1 score by a further 5%.

The characteristic microseismic difficulty is not one hard event, it is many weak ones. Multiple events inside a single window is common here and rare in global earthquake data — which is why picking models trained on catalogued earthquakes need re-tuning before they transfer.
Source: Anikiev et al. (2023), §2.3 & Fig. 3 · Thesis §2.4
Section 09 · Localisation

Locating without picking

Classical localisation runs on one of two tracks. Traveltime methods invert picked arrivals and tolerate a rough velocity model, because traveltimes average over the velocities they cross. Waveform methods — diffraction stacking, back-propagation, full-waveform grid search — need no picks at all but demand a velocity model accurate enough to model the waveform itself.

Machine learning enters at four distinct points, and they are worth separating:

  • 1
    Extracting the arrivals that a conventional inversion then uses — the network replaces the picker, not the physics.
  • 2
    Replacing the grid search itself: mapping raw or cross-correlated waveforms straight to a hypocentre, which removes the most expensive step and most of the operator-dependent pre-processing.
  • 3
    Returning a 3-D probability density instead of a point, so the answer carries its own uncertainty. Trained on synthetics with realistic noise, then transferred to field data — and extended by transfer learning to events weaker than those it was trained on.
  • 4
    Physics-informed networks that use the eikonal equation as the loss. Being continuous functions of space, they need no grid, so receivers along an irregular surface are no harder than a regular array.

Where methods have been compared directly on the same data, deep networks beat support vector machines and random forests. A full deep-learning location pipeline detected and located close to four times more events than conventional processing on the same records.

Surface micro-seismic arrays are usually single-component and the epicentres sit inside the array, so S-arrivals are hard to identify and most surface localisation runs on P-waves alone. That is a geometry limit, not an algorithm limit — and it caps what any model can recover.
Source: Anikiev et al. (2023), §2.4 & Fig. 4 · Thesis §2.5
Section 10 · Velocity

Everything rests on the velocity model

Every location, every moment tensor and every cluster interpretation inherits the error in the velocity model underneath it. Machine learning does not escape this. If the network is trained on synthetics, a velocity model was used to generate the labels; if it is trained on a historical catalogue, a velocity model was used to produce those locations in the first place.

There are only three ways to arrange the problem: invert for velocity with the sources known (the earthquake-seismology habit), invert for sources with the velocity known (the micro-seismic habit), or invert for both, jointly or in sequence.

The consequence is measurable. In a horizontal-well experiment where the true source position was known from a ball-seat event, a constant background velocity placed the event far too close to the wellbore. Inverting P- and S-velocities from the same passive recording moved it onto its true position and produced synthetic waveforms that matched the recorded ones.

Micro-seismic wavepaths run almost directly from a point source to the receiver. That geometry carries high-resolution velocity information — routinely discarded, and the most under-used data in the whole workflow.
Source: Anikiev et al. (2023), §2.8, Figs. 6–7 · Thesis §2.8
Case study 01 · Geology

Two carbonate fields, side by side

Gagarinskoe and Ozernoe sit in the north-eastern Solikamsk depression, with hydrocarbons held in a Famennian-age reef complex. They are close neighbours and deliberately unalike.

Gagarinskoe is shallower, hotter and more permeable: reservoir surface at −1778.3 m, initial pressure 20.7 MPa, 39 mD and 11% porosity, across a 12.3 m oil column. Ozernoe lies at −1997 m with 18.2 MPa, 31 mD and 9% porosity, but a thicker 19.8 m column.

X-ray microtomography of the cores shows pores and cavities; electron microscopy resolves microfractures and microcaverns. That void structure is not decoration — it is the template every induced fracture has to negotiate.

Source: Thesis §3.1 · Table 3.1, Figs. 3-3, 3-4
Finding 01 · Two wells

Eleven months, or eighteen

Well 1 was designed for a simple fracture and got one. POST-FRAC REPORT predicted a 72 m half-length; micro-seismic monitoring measured 73 m and the well test 75 m — a symmetric two-wing fracture about 15 m high, barely interacting with anything natural. Flow rose from 1.4 to 9.0 t/day, then decayed back to pre-treatment levels over 11 months as the inadequately propped fracture closed under overburden.

Well 2 was designed for 73 m and produced something else entirely: a multi-branched network with events scattered across a 100–350 m radius, and a well-test half-length of 382 m — five times the design. Fracture height reached 20 m. Flow went from 2.0 to 12.5 t/day and held, with minimal decline, for 18 months.

The separator is the storativity ratio (ω) from pressure-recovery analysis — how much fluid-storage volume the fracture system actually holds. Well 2's pressure curves show the double-porosity signature of the Warren–Root model: an interconnected network, not one plane.

Incremental recovery: 4.9% OIIP for the simple fracture against 17.7% OIIP for the complex network — in adjacent wells, in the same reservoir.
Source: Thesis §3.2.4 · Table 3.2, Figs. 3-7, 3-10, 3-11
Finding 02 · At field scale

The complexity premium

Two wells make an anecdote. The thesis therefore pools 109 hydraulic fracturing operations across both fields — 28 at Gagarinskoe, 81 at Ozernoe — and splits them by realised fracture geometry.

The result is unusually clean. Averaged across every operation, the immediate flow-rate gain is almost identical: 12.0 t/day for simple fractures, 12.2 t/day for complex ones. Complexity buys nothing on day one.

It buys everything afterwards. Effective duration runs 127 days for simple geometry against 311 days for complex — and cumulative additional oil follows it, 6.94 against 13.18 thousand tonnes.

Equal on arrival, 2.45× longer in effect, 1.9× more oil. The premium is entirely a duration effect, and duration tracks the storativity ratio linearly.
Source: Thesis §3.2.4 · Tables 3.3 & 3.4, Figs. 3-12 – 3-14
Case study 02 · The rock

Well X: a much harder problem

Well X targets the Dalong Formation in the Puguang region of the Sichuan Basin, with two targets at 4,565 m and 5,800 m and a 1,235 m horizontal section. Curvature attributes flag weak stratigraphic planes at 4,565–4,651 m and 4,865–4,968 m, where natural fractures concentrate.

The rock argues both ways. Porosity is punishing — 0.62 to 4.93%, averaging 2.61% — but gas content averages 5.14 m³/t with 71.58% of it free gas, which is what delivers rate after a frac. Mineralogy is favourable: silica and calcium together exceed 80% while clay averages just 4.28%, so the formation is brittle enough to shatter into a network rather than deform.

Against that: cohesion 13.12 MPa, internal friction angle 55.76°, tensile strength 12.47 MPa, fracture toughness 0.77 MPa·√m, and a closure stress of 96 MPa that threatens long-term conductivity. Maximum horizontal stress runs east–west, minimum north–south, confirmed by FMI drilling-induced fractures and breakouts.

Source: Thesis §3.3.1 · Table 3-5, Figs. 3-15 – 3-20
Case study 02 · Execution

Twenty-five stages against the pressure limit

The design answered the rock with scale: 25 stages of roughly 45 m, a few-cluster layout, high-viscosity pre-pad, integrated temporary plugging, and 55 m³/m of fluid at 2.5 m³/m proppant intensity.

In the field, wellhead pressure ran 96 to 112 MPa at 12–16 m³/min — pressed against the equipment limit, which is exactly why the rate could not be raised further. Total proppant reached 2,940 m³: 70/140 quartz sand first to polish the perforations and cut friction, then ceramic in 70/140 and 40/70 (about 80% of the total), with 30/50 used sparingly near the fracture entry for conductivity. Realised intensity ranged 1.6–3.4 m³/m, averaging 2.4.

Ground detectors in a star pattern showed propagation running predominantly westward, with fractures reaching 154–478 m long, 53–87 m wide and 25–48 m high — growing unevenly on either side of the trajectory.

Pre-flush matters more than expected: cross-linked slickwater drove vertical growth, and at least 20 m³ of polishing sand was needed before the benefit appeared at all.
Source: Thesis §3.3.2.1–3.3.2.3 · Figs. 3-21 – 3-25
Finding 03 · Diversion

Forcing complexity with plugs

Temporary plugging blocks perforations mid-stage so pressure redistributes and untouched clusters break down. Pressure limits meant it could not be used on every stage — which accidentally produced a controlled comparison.

Where plugging balls were dropped, operating pressure rose 1.32 to 5.5 MPa; particles gave 0.5 to 6.33 MPa. Micro-seismic response separates the two clearly: stages 11 to 17 generated markedly more events after plugging, several of them well down the trajectory, while the first nine and last five stages stayed sparse.

Balls outperformed particles. Particles mostly re-activated ground that was already stimulated; balls pushed propagation into new rock and spread it evenly across clusters.

Both signals agree — pressure rise and event count increase together. That coincidence is the evidence that the plug worked, not merely that pumping got harder.
Source: Thesis §3.3.2.4 · Fig. 3-26
Finding 04 · Classification

Grading a fracture network

"Complex" needs a threshold or it is just a compliment. The thesis builds one directly from the event cloud: connect adjacent micro-fractures of similar orientation, discard isolated ones, and measure how much of the micro-seismic footprint the resulting effective grid covers.

At 85% coverage or above the network counts as complex. Around 60% it is moderately complex. Below 60% it does not qualify, whatever the event count suggests.

Applying it to Well X separates engineering from geology. Stages 13 and 15 scored highest — and both sit beside pre-existing natural fractures near fault zones. Stages far from faults stayed low regardless of how they were pumped.

Geology is the prerequisite; engineering is the multiplier. Plugging, pre-flush and fluid design refine a complex network where the rock permits one — they cannot manufacture it where it does not.
Source: Thesis §3.3.2.5, §3.3.3 · Fig. 3-27
Case study 03 · Qusaiba

Four hundred and fifteen events

A third rock, and a first: the Lower Silurian Qusaiba "hot shale" in north-western Saudi Arabia, monitored during the first multistage hydraulic stimulation ever carried out in the Arabian Peninsula. Twelve stages, plug-and-perf, along the horizontal section of a deviated well, pumped over ten days.

The array was downhole: 12 three-component sensors in a neighbouring vertical well at 30.5 m spacing, a 335 m aperture centred on the target level. Surface arrays recorded in parallel and were discarded — their signal-to-noise ratio was so poor that only perforation shots and the initial breakdown events registered.

Processing was deliberately conservative: DC-offset removal, a 20–600 Hz bandpass, then a double-window trigger function scanning the continuous stream. Locations came from a collapsing grid search — 30.5 m down to 10 m — with two-point ray tracing through a seven-layer velocity model built from a VSP survey in the monitoring well.

415 events were located. Every stage produced fractures at an average azimuth of N335° E, normal to the horizontal treatment well, which says the local stress direction does not rotate along the wellbore — neither naturally nor under injection. Stages 1 and 3 yielded nothing locatable at all.

A large share of triggers were tube waves: coherent, high-amplitude arrivals on all twelve tools with an impossibly slow apparent velocity, generated by fluid moving along the wellbore wall. Separating them from real events is a classification problem — precisely the task Chapter Two hands to machine learning.

Ellipsoids fitted to 90% of each stage's events give the stimulated volume and aspect ratio per stage. Early and late stages produced the longest networks; mid-stages propagated markedly less. Peak pressure and pump rate were near-identical across all twelve, so the difference is rock, not pumping. Clouds from stages 4–5, and more strongly 6–9, overlap — the induced networks interact.

Source: Kaka et al. (2017), Petroleum Geoscience · doi:10.1144/petgeo2016-086 · Figs. 4, 5, 7, 8
Finding 05 · Detection limit

What the array could actually hear

An event map is never a map of the fracturing. It is a map of the fracturing the array was able to detect, and this dataset measures its own limits unusually well.

Sensitivity falls by one magnitude unit every 375 m, and past roughly 700 m the array detects nothing at all. Any apparent absence of events beyond that radius is a statement about the instrument, not about the rock.

Location uncertainty was tested by Monte Carlo: 200 relocations at each of two test positions, with the velocity model randomly perturbed by 5% and by 10%. Vertical scatter is similar early and late. Horizontal scatter for the distant early stages is double that of the near late stages — so early-stage geometry is the least trustworthy part of the catalogue, which matters because it is exactly the part that appears to show the longest fractures.

The frequency–magnitude distribution gives a b-value near 2.3, against roughly 1.0 for tectonic earthquakes. Small events dominate overwhelmingly: large pre-existing fractures play little part in the monitored seismicity. Energy partition supports it — the S-to-P energy ratio stays above the tensile threshold of 10 throughout, so rupture is shear-dominated. The apparent drift toward a tensile signature late in the job is an attenuation artefact: P-waves carry higher frequencies, attenuate faster, and the later stages sit closer to the array.

The honest reading of 415 events: cloud geometry, aspect ratio and array-corrected magnitude distribution are robust enough to validate a geomechanical model. Individual event positions and any temporal trend in b-value are not.
Source: Kaka et al. (2017), Figs. 6, 9, 10 · median SNR 1.66, median location residual 2.5 m
Section 20 · Conclusion

A framework for the field

Micro-seismic monitoring turns out to be less a verification tool than a design instrument. It maps what actually formed, corrects volume estimates that models get wrong, catches unintended growth toward faults, and — paired with well testing — explains why one well holds its rate and its neighbour does not.

Machine learning is what makes it routine rather than specialist: automated detection and classification, noise suppression that filtering cannot achieve, and wavefield reconstruction from gapped data. The Qusaiba dataset shows why that matters even when the array is good — 415 events, a median signal-to-noise ratio of 1.66, and enough tube-wave triggers that most phases had to be checked by hand.

It also shows the discipline the method demands. Every event map is bounded by the array that made it: one magnitude unit of sensitivity lost every 375 m, nothing at all past 700 m, and location scatter that doubles for the most distant stages. Read within those bounds, micro-seismic monitoring validates a geomechanical model. Read outside them, it invents fractures.

  1. Monitor every job. Real-time micro-seismic on all treatments, to sharpen SRV estimates and flag unintended growth.
  2. Automate the interpretation. Extend ML across detection, noise suppression and wavefield reconstruction.
  3. Design to the geology. Evaluate natural fractures before pumping; tune the treatment to them.
  4. Suppress noise properly. Low-frequency background and "dry" events are the main source of mapping error.
  5. Train and share. Operators, researchers and regulators need the same working vocabulary.
  6. Report the environmental record. Monitoring data is the strongest available evidence on containment — publish it.
